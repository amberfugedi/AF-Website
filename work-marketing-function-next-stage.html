Building a marketing function for its next stage of growth

A marketing team can be good at its work and still be built for the size the business used to be. This is the part underneath the campaigns: the roles, the operating model, the way performance is read, and the investment behind all of it.

Written without naming an organization. This is an account of how I do this work and the decisions behind it, drawn from leading marketing in-house. The market, the numbers and the timeline are left out deliberately, and nothing here reproduces internal documentation, workflows, templates or materials.

A growing business outgrows the shape its marketing was built for, and it usually happens while the campaigns are still working.

What lags is the structure underneath: roles drawn for an earlier size of company, operating knowledge living in individual heads, and a record of what has been spent and what it returned sitting in several places at once.

None of that is a failure. It is what happens when a function keeps delivering while the business around it changes shape. The question is never how to fix marketing. It is what marketing needs to become so the next stage of growth does not have to wait for it.

The transformation

The starting point The direction
  • Roles drawn for an earlier size of company Roles drawn for the stage the business is heading into
  • Operating knowledge living in individual heads One operating model the team helped shape and can hand on
  • A record of spend and return sitting in several places Performance in one place, with the age of every figure visible
  • A marketing plan written first A marketing plan written from the business plan, and shorter for it
  • Investment argued case by case An investment case in the same language as every other commercial one

Five workstreams, and what each one turned on

  1. Marketing strategy and business alignment

    Start from the business plan, not the marketing plan. The first question was where the company intended to be, and what it would need from marketing to get there.

    The marketing plan came second, and it was shorter for it.

  2. Organizational design and team development

    Design the roles before filling them. It is tempting to hire for the work in front of you. I drew the roles the function would need at the next stage, then worked out which of them existed, which could be grown into, and which had to be brought in.

    People develop inside roles that have somewhere to go, rather than leaving to find the next step.

  3. Marketing operations and the operating model

    Write the operating model down, with the people who use it. A model written alone is a document. I designed it and put it in place together with the person who would run it every day, so the team shaped the thing they were going to work inside.

    A model shaped that way survives a handover, which is what makes it possible to prioritize work, pass it on, or stop it on purpose.

  4. Marketing intelligence and performance

    Make performance readable before asking anyone to act on it. Numbers that take a week to assemble do not get used. Performance has to be something a leader can open and trust, with the history beside it and no question about how current it is.

    Conversations then start from the evidence rather than from assembling it.

  5. Investment and planning

    Put the investment case in the same language as the rest of the business. Benchmarking against previous years, modelling how different allocations would perform, and running scenarios against the revenue target.

    A marketing decision can then be weighed the way any other commercial one is. That matters more than it sounds: marketing budgets get cut when nobody outside marketing can explain them.

Growth puts weight on the parts of a function nobody looks at.

More pipeline means more handoffs. More people means more places for context to go missing. A new segment means the old definitions stop fitting. The work that makes a function hold at the next stage is almost never the campaign work, which is exactly why it gets deferred.

That is the argument for doing it early. A function rebuilt while things are going well is a planning exercise. The same work started once growth has already outpaced the structure is a rescue, and it costs a year.

Leading the function. Staying close to the work.

I sat in the rooms where the rest of the business made its plans, which is the part that changes how the job is done. Marketing decisions made next to the commercial ones are different decisions.

Strategic leadership

Where the function is going, and what it costs.

  • Business planning and prioritization
  • Budget allocation and scenario planning
  • Executive alignment
  • Team leadership and role design

Hands-on execution

Close enough to know whether any of it is landing.

  • Demand generation
  • Positioning and messaging
  • Campaign development and websites
  • Sales enablement and marketing systems

These are not two jobs. When the resources, tools or processes do not exist yet, the person setting the direction is often the fastest person to build the first version of them, and the direction gets better for having been near the work.

I used to measure a year by what marketing produced. I measure it now by what the function can do that it could not do before, and by how many of the people in it are ready for a job bigger than the one they have.

Most of my attention goes to the conditions around the work rather than the work itself.
