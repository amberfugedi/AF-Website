I’ve never been very good at staying inside the marketing box.

I’m Amber. I’ve spent more than a decade in marketing, but the work I’m most drawn to usually starts somewhere between marketing, sales, operations, technology, and the question nobody has asked yet.

Marketing leader. Strategist. Builder. Constant question-asker.

I usually start with the problem underneath the problem.

Give me a website problem and I’ll probably start asking about sales.

Tell me you need more leads and I’ll want to know what happens to them after they come in.

Show me a process everyone hates and I’m going to wonder why everyone is still doing it.

I like figuring out what is actually going on, then making it better.

I came up in marketing by doing the work.

Websites. Campaigns. Content. Demand generation. CRM. Reporting. Events. Sales enablement. Positioning. The things that looked straightforward on a plan and became considerably less straightforward once real people had to use them.

Over time, my job got bigger. I started leading teams, working more closely with sales and executives, making decisions about systems and budgets, and seeing how often a “marketing problem” was really connected to something happening somewhere else in the business.

That is probably the biggest influence on how I work now.

I don’t assume the first problem someone hands me is the real one. I ask questions. I look at the numbers. I talk to the people doing the work. I look at what customers are actually doing. And then I try to find the simplest useful answer.

Sometimes that answer is a campaign.

Sometimes it is a new website.

Sometimes it is fixing what happens after someone fills out the form.

And sometimes the answer is that we should stop doing something altogether.

What I keep coming back to.

Facts over feelings.

I like opinions. I just like them more when we can test them.

What are customers actually doing? Where are leads actually dropping off? What is sales actually hearing? What does the data say compared with what we assumed was happening?

The interesting part usually starts when those answers do not match.

My idea is not the only idea.

The person doing the work every day knows things I don’t. Sales hears the objection I never hear. Whoever answers the support tickets knows which promise breaks first.

So I would rather be argued with early than agreed with politely. A plan nobody pushed on usually has a soft spot in it somewhere, and I would rather find that in the room than after it launches.

Some of the best decisions I have been part of started as someone else’s objection.

Strategy only matters if it gets built.

A recommendation that never meets a real constraint tends to stay on the page.

Once you write the actual words, build the workflow, launch the campaign, or explain the change to the person who has to live with it, you find out which parts of the strategy were actually true.

That is why I like staying close to the work.

Make the work lighter.

Most businesses carry around work nobody really needs, and nobody has been told they can stop doing.

Extra approvals. Duplicated documents. Reports nobody reads. Processes built around tools the team stopped using two years ago.

I’m always looking for some version of: can this be simpler? AI can help where the work repeats. Everywhere else, it can just as easily add another step.

The real test is whether the team still uses what we built after I leave.

I’m a strategy person who still wants to touch the work.

I like the big-picture part of marketing. Figuring out where the business is going, what needs to change, how the pieces connect, and what is worth prioritizing.

But I have never wanted to become the person who only hands someone else the deck.

I still like rewriting the headline.

I still want to look at the landing page.

I’ll probably notice the strange field in the CRM.

I want to understand why a workflow has twelve steps when it could have four.

And if we decide something needs to change, I like being close enough to the team to make sure it actually does.

That tends to mean I end up doing things like
  • Improving websites
  • Writing messaging and positioning
  • Building demand generation programs
  • Creating content and campaigns
  • Fixing workflows and reporting
  • Improving CRM processes
  • Designing practical AI automations
  • Connecting marketing and sales
  • Working alongside the team through rollout

The people part matters to me, too.

One of the things I’ve made time for throughout my career is mentoring.

I got meaningful experience early in my own career, and it put me ahead in ways I didn’t fully appreciate at the time. Someone trusted me with real work before I had years of experience proving I could do it.

I’ve tried to pass that on.

I’ve worked with interns and people early in their careers for years, and I care a lot about giving people more than a list of tasks. I want them in the conversation. I want them to hear how decisions get made, ask questions, try things, get feedback, and leave knowing more than they did when they started.

I think good leadership should make people better at what they do, not more dependent on the person leading them.

From people I've worked with.

And outside the marketing of it all…

I’m based in Connecticut, usually have a dog somewhere nearby, and have a hard time turning off the part of my brain that notices how things could work better.

I’m curious about a lot more than marketing. I like learning how people think, why businesses work the way they do, new technology, psychology, design, and probably too many other rabbit holes to list here.

I also genuinely enjoy making things.

Sometimes that is a campaign.

Sometimes it is a website.

Sometimes it is a system that saves someone three hours every week.

And sometimes it is changing a tiny piece of copy because I’ve been thinking about it for two days and finally figured out why it was bothering me.
