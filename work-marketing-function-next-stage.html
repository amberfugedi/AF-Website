Building a marketing function for its next stage of growth

A marketing team can be good at its work and still be built for the size the business used to be. This case study is about the part underneath the campaigns: the roles, the operating model, the way performance is read, and the investment behind all of it.

Written without naming an organization. This is an account of how I do this work and the decisions behind it, drawn from leading marketing in-house. The market, the numbers and the timeline are left out deliberately, and nothing here reproduces internal documentation, workflows, templates or materials.

Context
Senior in-house marketing leadership
Organization
Not named
Role
Owner of the marketing function
Focus
Organizational design, operating model, marketing intelligence, investment planning

The business context

A growing business outgrows the shape its marketing was built for, and it usually happens while the campaigns are still working. What lags is the structure underneath: roles drawn for an earlier size of company, operating knowledge living in individual heads, and a record of what has been spent and what it returned sitting in several places at once.

None of that is a failure. It is what happens when a function keeps delivering while the business around it changes shape. The question is never how to fix marketing. It is what marketing needs to become so the next stage of growth does not have to wait for it.

What I owned

The function end to end: brand and positioning, demand generation, marketing operations, and the enablement work that sits between marketing and sales. I created the roles the business needed and developed the people in them, directed where the investment went, and built the internal tooling that made performance easier to read.

I also sat in the rooms where the rest of the business made its plans, which is the part that changes how the job is done. Marketing decisions made next to the commercial ones are different decisions.

The decisions that mattered

  1. Start from the business plan, not the marketing plan.The first question was where the company intended to be, and what it would need from marketing to get there. The marketing plan came second, and it was shorter for it.
  2. Design the roles before filling them.It is tempting to hire for the work in front of you. I drew the roles the function would need at the next stage, then worked out which of them existed, which could be grown into, and which had to be brought in.
  3. Write the operating model down, with the people who use it.A model written alone is a document. I designed it and put it in place together with the person who would run it every day, so the team shaped the thing they were going to work inside.
  4. Make performance readable before asking anyone to act on it.Numbers that take a week to assemble do not get used. Performance has to be something a leader can open and trust, with the history beside it and no question about how current it is, before anyone can be asked to make a call on it.
  5. Put the investment case in the same language as the rest of the business.Benchmarking against previous years, modelling how different allocations would perform, and running scenarios against the revenue target, so a marketing decision could be weighed the way any other commercial one is.

What it builds

A function built this way holds its shape as the business grows. The roles have somewhere to go, so people develop inside them instead of leaving to find the next step. The operating model survives a handover, which means work can be prioritized, passed on, or stopped on purpose rather than by attrition. Performance is legible to anyone who needs it, so conversations start from evidence instead of from assembling it. And the investment has a case behind it that someone can still follow a year later.

That last one matters more than it sounds. Marketing budgets get cut when nobody outside marketing can explain them.

Why this matters when a business grows

Growth puts weight on the parts of a function nobody looks at. More pipeline means more handoffs. More people means more places for context to go missing. A new segment means the old definitions stop fitting. The work that makes a function hold at the next stage is almost never the campaign work, which is exactly why it gets deferred.

A note on leading this kind of change

I used to measure a year by what marketing produced. I measure it now by what the function can do that it could not do before, and by how many of the people in it are ready for a job bigger than the one they have. Most of my attention goes to the conditions around the work rather than the work itself.
