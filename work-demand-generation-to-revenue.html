Connecting demand generation to revenue

Marketing can hit every number it sets for itself and still not be trusted by the people carrying the revenue target. This case study is about closing that gap: working with Sales toward one commercial outcome rather than two parallel ones.

Written without naming an organization. This is an account of how I do this work and the decisions behind it, drawn from leading marketing in-house. The market, the numbers and the timeline are left out deliberately, and nothing here reproduces internal documentation, workflows, templates or materials.

Context
Senior in-house marketing leadership
Organization
Not named
Role
Owner of demand generation and marketing investment
Focus
Demand generation, Sales partnership, pipeline performance, investment planning

The business context

Marketing and sales usually measure different things. Marketing counts what it generates. Sales counts what it closes. Both sets of numbers are accurate, and neither one answers the question the business actually has, which is whether the money going into demand generation is turning into revenue.

The gap is structural rather than anyone’s fault: it is what you get when two functions are measured on their own outputs. It is also expensive, because it turns every investment conversation into an argument about whose numbers are real. Closing it is what this work is for.

What I owned

Demand generation end to end, the marketing investment behind it, and the enablement work that sits between the two functions. The job was to make marketing answerable for commercial outcomes rather than for marketing outcomes, and then to build the demand programs that could actually carry that.

Working with Sales

This is the part that does the most work, and the part that is hardest to shortcut. I work closely with Sales and the other business leaders to connect demand generation, pipeline performance and marketing investment to shared commercial goals. In practice that means a few things.

It means agreeing what counts before the quarter starts rather than arguing about it afterwards, so a lead, an opportunity and a sourced deal mean the same thing on both sides of the house. It means marketing looking at the same pipeline Sales looks at, in the same view, so a disagreement about performance is a disagreement about one set of figures instead of two. It means marketing being present for the conversations about what is actually closing and why, because the reasons deals stall are the best demand generation brief there is. And it means marketing carrying part of the commercial number, not a separate one that can be hit in a quarter the business misses.

What it does not mean is paperwork between the two teams. The useful version of this is a shared view and a shared goal, not a contract.

The decisions that mattered

  1. Measure marketing on sourced revenue, not on volume.Lead counts reward the cheapest lead. Sourced bookings reward the right one. Changing what marketing is judged on changes what marketing builds long before any campaign does.
  2. Agree the definitions once, in public.Most of the friction between marketing and sales is two teams using the same five words to mean different things.
  3. Follow the money through the whole path, not to the handoff.A program that produces a lot of pipeline and very little revenue is a program that is working perfectly and should be stopped.
  4. Spend where the evidence points, including away from marketing’s own favorites.The investment analysis is only credible if it is allowed to recommend cutting something marketing likes.

What it builds

When this works, the arguments change. Sales stops asking marketing for more leads and starts asking for a specific kind of conversation. Marketing stops defending its volume and starts defending its allocation. The investment case for next year is built on what the business already learned this year, in numbers both functions recognize.

The quieter outcome is that marketing stops having to justify itself every quarter. When the number it reports is a number the business already uses, the conversation moves on to what to do next.

A note on working this way

The marketing was never the hard part. The hard part was getting two functions to agree on what good looks like, and then being willing to be measured against it. I would rather own a smaller number that the business believes than a larger one it argues with.
